# General Cyber Hygiene Checklist

**Document Version:** 1.0  
**Target Audience:** Small to Medium Enterprises (SMEs), IT Teams, Operations Managers  
**Objective:** A foundational baseline to protect company systems, data, and employee accounts from common digital threats.

---

## Why Cyber Hygiene Matters
Just like personal hygiene keeps you healthy, cyber hygiene refers to the fundamental practices organizations perform regularly to keep their networks secure. Most security breaches happen not from advanced attacks, but because basic safeguards—like updating software or changing default passwords—were overlooked.

---

## 1. Device and Asset Management
You cannot protect equipment or software if you do not know you own it. The first step to safety is knowing exactly what is running on your network.

- [ ] **Create a Hardware Inventory:** Write down a complete list of all laptops, desktops, servers, tablets, and smartphones used for business purposes. Note who uses each device and its physical location.
- [ ] **Create a Software Inventory:** Make a list of all approved software applications, mobile apps, and cloud tools used by your employees. Uninstall any software that is outdated or no longer needed for business.
- [ ] **Secure Guest Wi-Fi Networks:** Ensure that office visitors use a dedicated "Guest" Wi-Fi network that is completely separated from the primary network where your company computers and shared files sit.

## 2. User Accounts and Password Security
Weak or stolen credentials are the most common way unauthorized individuals gain access to business networks. Securing user identities is your strongest line of defense.

- [ ] **Enforce Strong Passwords:** Require all employees to use passwords that are at least 12 characters long and include a mix of uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols. Ban the use of easily guessable passwords (like `Password123` or the company's name).
- [ ] **Turn on Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):** Enable MFA (sometimes called two-step verification) on all critical business accounts. Priority should be given to company email, VPNs, accounting software, and cloud storage. 
- [ ] **Remove Default Passwords:** Change the default factory passwords on all new office equipment—especially Wi-Fi routers, firewalls, printers, and security cameras—before plugging them into the network.
- [ ] **Practice the Principle of Least Privilege:** Employees should only have access to the specific files, folders, and software required to do their jobs. Do not give standard users "Administrator" rights on their daily work laptops.
- [ ] **Clean Up Dormant Accounts:** Review user accounts monthly. Immediately deactivate accounts belonging to former employees or vendors who no longer work with the business.

- [ ] ## 3. Software Updates and Patch Management
Software companies regularly release updates to fix security flaws. If you don't install these updates, your systems remain vulnerable to known entry methods.

- [ ] **Turn on Automatic Updates:** Configure all laptops, desktops, and mobile devices to automatically download and install operating system updates (such as Windows Update or macOS Updates) as soon as they are released.
- [ ] **Keep Web Browsers and Apps Updated:** Ensure that everyday applications—especially web browsers (Chrome, Edge, Safari), PDF readers, and office suites—are kept on the latest versions.
- [ ] **Update Network Hardware Firmware:** Check your office Wi-Fi routers, network switches, and firewalls at least once a month for firmware updates, and apply them during non-business hours to avoid disruption.
- [ ] **Do Not Use Unsupported Software:** Identify any legacy operating systems (like Windows 7 or older server versions) or discontinued software applications that no longer receive security updates from the manufacturer, and plan to upgrade or replace them immediately.

- [ ] ## 4. Data Protection and Backup Routine
Data is the lifeblood of any business. If a disruption occurs, having safely stored copies of your operational records ensures you can recover without paying a ransom or facing operational ruin.

- [ ] **Follow the 3-2-1 Backup Rule:** Keep at least three (3) copies of your important business data: store two (2) copies on different types of media (such as a local external hard drive and an internal server) and keep one (1) copy completely off-site or in the cloud.
- [ ] **Isolate Your Backups (Air-Gapping):** Ensure that your primary backup drives or cloud folders are isolated from the main network. If a ransomware infection spreads across your office networks, it will try to encrypt backup files too if they are connected.
- [ ] **Automate the Process:** Do not rely on employees remembering to manually copy files to a thumb drive. Use automated backup software to run daily incremental backups and weekly full backups.
- [ ] **Test the Restoration Process:** A backup is only useful if it actually works. At least once every six months, practice restoring a sample folder or system database from your backups to verify that the data is not corrupted and can be recovered quickly.
- [ ] **Encrypt Sensitive Files:** Ensure that laptops or external drives carrying confidential client information or financial records use full-disk encryption (like BitLocker on Windows or FileVault on Mac) so that data remains unreadable if a device is physically lost or stolen.

- [ ] ## 5. Employee Awareness and Daily Habits
The human element is often the target of cyber attacks. Training your team to spot common traps creates a strong human firewall for the organization.

- [ ] **Conduct Phishing Awareness Training:** Teach employees how to spot suspicious emails. Look out for urgent requests for financial transfers, suspicious email sender addresses, mismatched link URLs, and unexpected attachments.
- [ ] **Establish Safe Browsing Habits:** Advise employees never to download files, browser extensions, or software tools from untrusted or pop-up websites on work computers.
- [ ] **Enforce Clear Desk and Screen Rules:** Train staff to manually lock their computer screens (`Windows Key + L` on Windows) every single time they step away from their desks, even for a brief moment.
- [ ] **Report Incidents Immediately:** Create a culture where employees are comfortable reporting mistakes—such as accidentally clicking a suspicious link or entering credentials on a strange page—without fear of punishment, so the IT team can contain the threat quickly.

---

##  Routine Maintenance Schedule
To keep this checklist effective, establish a recurring review cycle:
* **Monthly:** Review inactive accounts, verify backup automation logs, and check network hardware for firmware updates.
* **Quarterly:** Review software inventories and verify patch management cycles.
* **Annually:** Test full-system data restorations and refresh employee phishing awareness training.
